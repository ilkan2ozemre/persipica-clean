How Pulse measures AI answers

What Pulse asks, how it collects each answer, what a percentage means, and what a scan can't tell you.

Last updated 10 October 2026

What Pulse asks

You tell Pulse who you sell to. It drafts questions the way each kind of shopper would ask them, in their own words: "which merino socks stop blisters on long walks?" rather than a keyword. You approve the shopper types it asks as, and you can add questions of your own.

  • 27 questions a scan by default. You can set anywhere from 9 to 45.
  • Most questions don't name you. That is how shoppers find products they haven't heard of, so it's what "how often AI recommends you" is measured on. Questions that do name you, such as comparisons, are kept apart and never count towards that rate.
  • The shoppers are simulated. They are profiles used to phrase questions, not real people or customer data. Each attribute is labelled as your data, a public signal or an assumption, and results always say they come from simulated shoppers.

How answers are collected

Pulse covers seven assistants: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Perplexity and Grok, plus Google AI Overviews and Google AI Mode.

  • Consumer interfaces. Pulse reads the chat assistants through their consumer interfaces, so it sees answers the way a shopper does, with each assistant's own live web search. It can also collect through each assistant's API.
  • Google's AI. AI Overviews and AI Mode are read as they appear in Google search results, once per question.
  • Repeated asking. The same question can get a different answer each time, so each question goes to each chat assistant three times.
  • Follow-ups. Where a shopper would ask a follow-up, it continues the same conversation, with the earlier turns included.
  • Language. Questions are asked, and answers read, in the language you choose for the scan.

What a percentage means

Every result shows three things, kept apart so one can't hide another.

  • Coverage: how many answers arrived. For example, "60 of 81 answers usable, 21 timed out". It's shown whenever any answers are missing, with the reasons.
  • Identity: which mentions are really you. Mentions Pulse couldn't attribute with confidence are counted as unresolved and shown next to the score, never quietly dropped or counted against you.
  • The rate: how often you're named. The share of usable, resolved answers that name you. If 60 answers were resolved and 30 named you, the rate is 30 of 60.

Every rate comes with a likely range, written as "8 of 72, likely 6–20%". It is a 95% Wilson interval, which stays accurate near 0% and 100% and with small samples. It describes the variation you'd expect from asking again. It doesn't account for answers that never arrived, which is why coverage is shown separately.

Answers to the same question tend to look alike, so Pulse works out a rate for each question first and then averages them. One question asked many times counts as one question, which keeps the range from looking more certain than the evidence is.

When results are combined across assistants, each assistant's weight is fixed before the scan runs. An assistant that answered more often doesn't gain extra influence over the overall score.

When an answer doesn't arrive

Assistants sometimes time out, hit rate limits or show a CAPTCHA. Pulse plans every answer a scan should collect before it starts, and records what happened to each one.

  • Retries. Each request gets a limited number of attempts, with spaced, randomised waits, and Pulse follows an assistant's own instructions about when to try again. Errors that will never succeed, such as a rejected key, are not retried.
  • Pauses, not losses. If an assistant keeps failing, Pulse pauses it briefly and holds the remaining questions instead of marking them as failed. It tests the assistant with a few requests before resuming.
  • Retry what's missing. You can collect missing answers again without rerunning the ones that arrived. Answers collected after the scan has closed are labelled as a later collection, not mixed into the original.
  • Blank answers, refusals and "none of these". A blank response is left out of the rate and counted. A refusal is recorded as a refusal. An answer that recommends no products is a real answer and counts as one.
  • Conversations that break off. If a follow-up fails, the earlier answers are kept and the conversation is marked incomplete. It still counts for what the shopper was first told, but not for results that need the whole conversation, such as a final choice.
  • Scans that come back empty. A scan is reserved when it starts and only counted against your allowance when it completes. If no usable answers come back, it isn't counted.

How Pulse knows an answer means you

  • A fast first pass. Pulse looks for your brand name, any other names you've given it and your website's domain, as whole words. A product name counts only when your brand appears in the same sentence, product card or cited page.
  • A check on what each mention refers to. Pulse compares each mention with your brand profile: your names, domains, products, category and locations, plus companies known to share your name. A link to your own domain settles it; a capital letter alone doesn't.
  • A closer look when it's unclear. Mentions that are still uncertain are checked in context. Pulse records the decision with the exact words it relied on, and confirms those words are really in the answer. If it still can't tell, the mention stays unresolved.
  • Name mix-ups, with evidence. The report names the other companies or words your name collides with and shows the answers affected. If the check can't run, it says "not assessed", never "low risk".
  • Your corrections. If you correct a decision, Pulse keeps the original alongside your change and updates the affected results.

What a scan doesn't tell you

  • It's a sample. Rates describe the questions in your scan, not every question shoppers ask. Different questions can give different results.
  • It's a snapshot. Assistants change their answers from week to week. Regular scans show the trend; one scan shows a moment.
  • Real shoppers' answers can differ. Assistants can tailor answers to a person's account, location and past chats, which no outside tool can see.
  • Mentions aren't sales. A rate shows how often AI names you, not what shoppers buy. Visits and conversions from AI assistants come from your own analytics, which Pulse can bring in on Business.

Check it yourself

Every answer behind every number is kept. Open any result to read the exact answers, which assistant gave them, when, and the sources it cited. If anything here is unclear, or you want to see it on your own brand, get in touch.
